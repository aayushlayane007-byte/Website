Year-in-Review: 2024-2025

Prompt chosen: What specific experiences in the past year have had the most impact on your personal, academic, or professional goals and life trajectory? Which future experiences will further encourage this growth? Please articulate specific academic and/or professional goals for the next year.

Looking back on my first year at the University of Cincinnati, I think the biggest change was that I started to understand what engineering looks like outside of just classes and assignments. I came into UC interested in mechanical engineering, but I was still figuring out what areas I liked, how I worked best, and what kind of experiences would help me grow. A few experiences from this year helped me see that I learn best when I am actually building, testing, presenting, or working through a real problem instead of only studying the theory behind it.

1. Bearcat Motorsports

Bearcat Motorsports was one of my first chances to see an engineering project that was bigger than a class assignment. At first, it was honestly a little intimidating because many students already seemed comfortable with the car, the tools, and the design process. I had some experience with SolidWorks, but being around a Formula SAE team showed me that creating a design is only one part of engineering.

What stood out to me most was how many details go into even one component. A part cannot just look correct in CAD; it has to fit with the rest of the system, be manufacturable, and make sense for the actual use of the vehicle. I also saw how much feedback matters. Even if I did not always feel like the most experienced person in the room, I learned by watching how students explained their decisions, questioned each other's designs, and improved their work over time. This experience helped me become more comfortable being around technical discussions and made me want to keep building my own design and analysis skills.

2. ENED 1120 Autonomous Robot Project

The ENED 1120 autonomous robot project was another important experience because it gave me a more structured introduction to engineering problem-solving. Our Lego Mindstorms robot had to sort mixed waste streams for a mobile TDP plant while also navigating a five-degree grade. The project had budget limits, checkpoints, and design deadlines, so it forced our team to make decisions instead of endlessly changing the design.

I was most interested in the mechanical side of the project, especially thinking about how the lift arm would work repeatedly without using too much power or becoming unreliable. The robot completed seven out of ten autonomous runs, which was not perfect, but it showed me how testing and iteration can improve a design. This project also helped me realize that I enjoy work that combines mechanical design, coding, testing, and practical constraints. It made me more interested in learning simulation tools like ANSYS and COMSOL because I wanted to better understand how designs behave before they are actually built.

3. UC MarketHub Innovation Challenge

The UC MarketHub Innovation Challenge helped me grow in a different way. Instead of focusing only on the technical side of an idea, I had to think about how to explain the value of the idea to other people. Pitching an online marketplace for students required me to take a simple concept and turn it into a clear story with a problem, solution, and potential impact.

This experience taught me that communication is a major part of turning an idea into something useful. I had to think about what the audience would care about, how to make the idea practical, and how to explain benefits such as reducing resale waste and helping students save money. It also made me more interested in the business side of engineering because I saw that good ideas need more than technical logic; they also need clear presentation, user understanding, and a reason for people to care.

Cumulative Growth

Across these experiences, I noticed that I started becoming more comfortable with uncertainty. At the start of the year, I felt like I needed to know exactly what I was doing before getting involved. Over time, I learned that a lot of growth comes from entering situations where I am not fully confident yet and then learning through feedback, mistakes, and repetition.

I also became more aware of how broad mechanical engineering can be. It is not only about designing parts or solving equations; it also involves testing, communication, teamwork, business thinking, and understanding how one decision affects a larger system. This helped me see that I am most interested in engineering work that connects technical problem-solving with real applications.

Personally, my first year also taught me how important it is to manage my time and energy. Balancing coursework, projects, involvement, and adjusting to college life required me to become more organized. I started using tools like Notion and Reclaim to plan my work, but the bigger lesson was learning how to prioritize and stay consistent even when things felt busy.

Goals for 2025-2026

Technical depth: Over the next year, I want to continue developing stronger technical skills in design, simulation, and analysis. My goal is to complete COMSOL's Structural Mechanics self-paced course and apply what I learn to a mechanical design or analysis project. I also want to complete at least one MATLAB or Simulink mini-project related to vibration analysis and use it as an honors showcase or portfolio piece.

Leadership: I want to become more active in leadership roles within engineering and campus organizations. In Bearcat Motorsports, I hope to mentor newer students on CAD practices and contribute more confidently to design discussions. I also want to explore opportunities such as CEAS Tribunal or other student leadership positions where I can better understand how decisions are made at the college level.

Personal effectiveness: I want to continue developing habits that help me reflect and improve consistently. One specific goal is to keep a short weekly reflection log where I review what I learned, what I struggled with, and what I need to adjust. This connects directly to the purpose of the UHP learning portfolio because it encourages me to treat each experience as part of a larger growth process.

By this time next year, I hope to have a clearer sense of my direction as a mechanical engineering student. I want to keep building technical skills, stay involved in meaningful projects, and become better at connecting design, analysis, communication, and leadership. My first year at UC showed me that every experience does not need to be perfect to be valuable; it just needs to teach me something I can carry into the next one.

Prompt chosen: How have you grown as a developing professional within your field or fields of interest in the past year? Consider skills such as communication, collaboration, critical thinking, leadership, professionalism, or navigating different perspectives. Using specific examples, how have your experiences - academic, co-curricular, work, research, etc. - contributed to this growth? What strengths have you continued to build, and what areas would you like to develop further over the upcoming year?

Looking back on this past year, I can see that my professional growth came from being placed in different kinds of environments: work, teaching, research, and Honors experiences. At the beginning of the year, I was mainly focused on gaining experience, building my resume, and becoming more technically skilled. By the end of the year, I started thinking more carefully about what kinds of experiences actually fit my strengths, interests, and long-term goals.

1. Southland Holdings

My Project Engineering Co-op at Southland Holdings was one of the most important professional experiences of my year. I tracked over 100 submittals, reviewed construction drawings using Bluebeam and AutoCAD, and worked with large specification documents. This helped me understand how much professional engineering work depends on organization, documentation, and clear communication.

One of the hardest parts of the co-op was not always knowing exactly what was expected of me or how productive I was supposed to be. That taught me that structure is not always given in a workplace. Sometimes, I have to create it by asking better questions, clarifying priorities, and organizing my own work. One project I was especially proud of was using Python and AI-assisted methods to extract and organize information from 2,000+ page specification documents. The project received strong feedback, including from the Vice President of Water Resources, which showed me that technical skills like coding and automation can create real value when applied to practical problems.

2. Peer Teaching Assistant

My role as a Peer Teaching Assistant for Engineering Design Thinking helped me grow in communication and leadership. I coached students in calculus, physics, and problem-solving, helped with grading and review sessions, and supported exams for a large group of students. Teaching made me more confident in my own knowledge because I had to explain concepts clearly to students with different levels of understanding.

This role also helped me become more comfortable leading a room. I learned that communication is not just about knowing the answer, but about breaking down a problem in a way that actually helps someone else understand it.

3. Undergraduate Research

My undergraduate research work with Metasonics helped me build more technical depth. I worked on a 2-D pressure acoustics model in COMSOL, ran frequency sweeps, and compared simulation results with analytical resonances. This was challenging because I had to learn new tools, understand the theory, and work through unclear results.

Research made me more interested in technical work because it showed me how theory, simulation, and validation connect. It also taught me patience with open-ended problems, where there is not always a clear path to the answer.

4. Chicago and Miami

My self-designed Honors experiences in Chicago and Miami helped me grow in a different way. I planned both trips for groups of eight people, balancing cost, time, quality, activities, and logistics. I genuinely enjoy exploring new places, but these trips also showed me that I am good at planning under constraints and optimizing different factors at once.

Chicago and Miami also made me more open-minded. Both cities showed me how architecture, culture, public spaces, and environment shape the way people experience a place. These experiences reminded me that professional growth does not only happen in classrooms or workplaces. It also comes from learning how to observe, adapt, and understand different perspectives.

Cumulative Growth

Across these experiences, I built strengths in communication, organization, adaptability, technical problem-solving, and independence. Southland helped me understand ownership and workplace expectations. Teaching helped me become a clearer communicator. Research helped me develop patience with technical uncertainty. Chicago and Miami helped me connect planning, culture, and perspective to my personal growth.

Most importantly, this year helped me move from simply trying to gain experience to thinking more carefully about which experiences fit the future I want to build.

Goals for the Upcoming Year

For the upcoming year, my first goal is to secure a co-op that is more closely aligned with my interests, especially in mechanical engineering, technical analysis, design, testing, manufacturing, or process improvement. I also want to network more intentionally so I can better understand different career paths before narrowing my direction too early.

At the same time, I want to explore my growing interest in business and finance. I still want to build a strong engineering foundation, but I can also see myself using that foundation in a broader path involving consulting, finance, investment banking, or eventually the CFA track. To explore this, I hope to get involved with organizations such as Queen City Consulting, University Funding Board, and Bearcat Ventures.

Overall, my goal is to become more intentional. I want to keep building technical knowledge while also exploring the business side of my interests, so that my next experiences are not just resume builders, but meaningful steps toward a clearer professional direction.
